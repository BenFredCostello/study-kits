L1

Physical and cognitive human–robot interaction

3 marks every year
  • pHRI = physical human–robot interaction (forces and contact). cHRI = cognitive (intention, signals such as EMG and EEG). Part 3 is organised as: actuators (pHRI) → controllers (p/cHRI) → cognitive interfaces (cHRI).
  • Traditional control systems and actuators are ill-suited to interacting safely with humans (e.g. the human–robot collision video).
  • Elements of a (bio)mechatronic system: sensor → controller → actuator → real world. Controllers and actuators matter because they are the interface to the real world.
  • Biomechatronic controllers can't act as rigid positioning systems, because they interact with soft and hard objects, and they can't assume a predictable environment, because it may be unknown or change over time.
  • Exoskeleton physiological factors: kinematic compatibility and human–robot joint misalignment. More than 35% of orthotic devices are abandoned, so actuators must be light, unobtrusive and comfortable.

Requirements (learn this list)

Conventional design
  • Speed and torque, i.e. power
  • Weight and dimensions
Biomechatronic and medical devices
  • Ergonomics and comfort
  • Aesthetics
  • Weight
  • Ease of use (donning and doffing)
  • Stiffness vs compliance (loads applied to the human)
  • Inherent safety (low voltage, fail-safes, dead-man switch, E-stop)
  • Backdrivability
  • Robots as tools for: therapy (stretching, strengthening, range of motion, functional tasks), assistance (augmenting strength, muscle control, activities of daily living, replacing function) and diagnosis (neuromuscular characteristics, clinical biomarkers, prevention and prediction).
  • McDaid's examples: an elbow exoskeleton with gamification; a wrist exoskeleton (3-D scanned and printed, adjusts for misalignment, easy to put on and take off, patented); an over-ground lower-limb gait trainer (multi-objective design optimisation, force fields that converge to natural gait); a passive assist-as-needed gait trainer for acute stroke; soft robotics (pneumatic muscles, SMA wrist exoskeleton); 4D-printed smart skin for osseointegrated prostheses (sensing infection by "smell"); a wearable neuroprosthesis (FES) for hand function.
  • OPUM Digital Knee: a remote, clinically accurate digital goniometer (a patented polycentric knee sensor that clips onto braces) with a patient app and clinician portal. In a 12-week RCT for knee osteoarthritis (Shirley Ryan AbilityLab) it improved pain and function, raised patient retention to 89%, increased brace wear from 5.6 to 8.41 h/day, and 67% of patients said they were less likely to have a knee replacement. Fios Health makes a digital medical assistant (an AI agent for clinic staff and patients).
  • Key lessons: get clinical and user input early ("engineers need clinicians"). The journey is long (be prepared), complex (experience matters) and full of setbacks (be resilient), but highly rewarding.
L2

Series elastic, variable stiffness and variable damping actuators

6 marks
  • Stiff actuator: moves to and holds a position whatever the external force (within its limits). Compliant actuator: allows deviation from its equilibrium position (the position where it produces zero force) depending on the external force. Stiffness is the opposite of compliance; think Hooke's law, F = k(x − x₀).
  • High stiffness gives precision, high gearing (torque), high bandwidth. Low stiffness feels spongy and is harder to control precisely at speed, because it adds a low resonance.
  • Why use compliance (2024 Q2): to limit large forces from shocks and uncertain environments; for safe pHRI; for comfort; to store and release energy in elastic elements; and to improve energy efficiency by matching the actuator's resonant frequency.
  • Passive compliance: naturally compliant materials (e.g. EAPs) or a spring. It's inherently safer (doesn't rely on power), stores energy, and can be fixed or variable. Active compliance: a stiff actuator plus a sensor and controller that mimic a spring. Everything must be fast enough; compliance can be changed online, but the limited controller bandwidth means it can't absorb shocks.
  • SEA: a fixed-stiffness spring in series between the motor or hydraulics and the load. The spring's inertia is much lower than the gearbox and motor's. Fixed compliance means it can't change the system's natural frequency. The spring deflection also gives a force measurement: Ts = k(θl − θm).
  • VSA: an elastic element plus a way to adjust its stiffness, so k is variable. SEA vs VSA (2025 Q2): an SEA's spring stiffness is fixed; a VSA's can be changed, so it can adapt compliance and natural frequency to the task.
  • Four VSA principles:
    1. Equilibrium-controlled: a fixed spring whose equilibrium position is moved (effectively an SEA with a controller).
    2. Antagonistic-controlled: two non-linear springs or actuators pulling against each other, like the biceps and triceps. Co-contraction stiffens the joint; moving both together shifts the position. Every human joint has at least 2 muscles. (Biceps contracts and triceps relaxes = flexed; the reverse = extended; both contract = stiffened; both relax = relaxed.)
    3. Structure-controlled: change the spring's geometry, e.g. rotate a beam to change its second moment of area (I = width × thickness³/12), use a laminated structure, or the "Jack Spring" with active and inactive coils.
    4. Mechanically controlled: change the preload or the effective lever arm, e.g. the VS-Joint with a cam disk and rollers, or a mechanical impedance adjuster.
  • Active-compliance examples: the da Vinci surgical robot (Maxon motors), Lokomat, Armeo Power.
  • Applications: bio-inspired actuators, humanoid bipedal walking, exoskeletons, prosthetics, robots that operate safely near humans.

Variable damping

  • Damping dissipates stored oscillation energy, through friction (gears, Bowden cables, joints) or viscous drag. It's velocity-related and can be linear or non-linear.
  • Varying damping changes the resonance properties for pHRI: it can vary responsiveness, allow low stiffness while staying stable, shorten settling time, and add resistance for exercise. Like stiffness, it can be controlled actively or passively.
  • MR (magnetorheological) fluid: micron-sized iron particles whose rheology (viscosity) changes with a magnetic field. Uses: vehicle seat suspension, clutches, seismic dampers, beam vibration dampers, a "seal-less" small damper (0 to ±125 N over ±3 mm, can also lock).
  • Case study: MR knee prosthesis (Herr and Wilkenfeld, 2003; the basis of Össur's Rheo Knee). It's self-contained: MR fluid in shear mode, angle sensor (potentiometer), strain gauges for axial load (is the foot on the ground?) and torque, electronics and battery, 1.4 kg in total. A state machine follows the 5 gait states and applies a different damping in each. It starts stiff in state 1 and compliant in state 2; after 20 steps it adapts to the user (B = A·Fmax). Pre-swing (state 3) has zero active damping. The swing states (4 and 5) adapt damping to walking speed and foot-contact time.

L4

Active controllers for compliant pHRI

2–5 marks
  • Controller hierarchy: traditional (motion and force) → interaction controllers → advanced interaction controllers based on health outcomes. Low-level controllers regulate state and behaviour (position, force) for safe, efficient physical interaction. High-level controllers generate the low-level inputs that produce the desired interactions.
  • Impedance (Z): velocity (motion) in, force out. Admittance (Y): force in, velocity (motion) out.
  • Key pHRI rule: the human and robot must physically complement each other. If one is an impedance (takes motion, gives force), the other must be an admittance, and vice versa. The interaction is a dynamic balance of the masses and compliances of both "systems". The robot's compliance can be varied to dominate or yield, and cognitive information can be used to set the robot's impedance.
  • Feedforward (based on a model; must anticipate the action) vs feedback (acts on error; copes with model error and perturbations).
  • Taxonomy of low-level pHRI control: feedback (hybrid force/position, made of force and position control; impedance control, made of damping and stiffness control) and feedforward (model-based, e.g. repetitive control).
  • Control design procedure: 1) specify the desired behaviour; 2) select actuators and sensors; 3) model the plant; 4) design the control law; 5) analyse and simulate; 6) implement in hardware.
  • Modelling: keep the essential effects and discard insignificant ones in the range of interest. A more accurate model isn't necessarily better. Include parametric and non-parametric uncertainties for robust or adaptive design. Open-loop control would be perfect only with a perfect model and no disturbances, so in practice closed loop is needed. The minimum-jerk trajectory is good for pHRI.
  • A 2nd-order system can be over-, under- or critically damped. Any passive physical system (m, b, k > 0) is stable.
  • Why model and compensate (2025 Q6): gravity compensation cancels the limb or robot weight; the model-based part cancels the plant's own dynamics (m, b, k), so the servo gains can be tuned independently of the system (e.g. for critical damping); it improves tracking and transparency; and it means lower feedback gains, which makes interaction safer and more compliant.
  • Why traditional motion control isn't suitable (2025 Q5): high-gain position control rejects external forces, so when a human pushes back it fights them (unsafe and uncomfortable; human and robot want different motions). It assumes a predictable environment, and contact with soft or hard objects needs force or interaction to be controlled.
L5

Active interaction control

2–3 marks
  • The two extremes: free space (all forces zero, so pure motion control) and stuck to a wall (position constrained, so control force). Biomechatronic systems sit in between.
  • High-gain motion control is good for strict tracking and disturbance rejection. The higher the gain, the less the system responds to external forces. Once contact is made, position control alone isn't enough.
  • The solution is to control the force or interaction directly, giving the interaction specific controlled properties, as our bodies do (gait states, golf swing).
  • Force control: control the force on the environment, fe = kex. Sensed forces are noisy (strain gauges), so derivatives aren't practical, and the desired force is usually constant. The practical law uses force error plus velocity damping (see Equations).
  • Impedance control (two interaction controller architectures, 2024 Q5) sets a target relationship between interaction force and position: Mdë + Ddė + Kde = Fext. It's an inner force loop with an outer position loop. Stable with stiff environments, but poor accuracy in free space because of friction and unmodelled dynamics.
  • Admittance control: measure the force and compute the motion. The plant is position-controlled (so behaves as an impedance) and an outer admittance loop generates xd. Accurate in free space, but can be unstable with stiff environments.
  • Tele-impedance: combines kinematic sensors with EMG to set the robot's impedance from the user's muscle activity.
  • Traditional hybrid control: control force in some directions and position in the others, with selection matrices S and S′ (e.g. industrial grinding). Biomechatronic hybrid control: continually switch between impedance and admittance modes so performance stays good whatever the environment's stiffness.
L6

Advanced control for health outcomes

4–5 marks
  • In biomechatronics, the end goal is often a health outcome, not regulating motion or force. Active patient participation maximises the benefit of therapy.
  • Rehabilitation after stroke or spinal cord injury must be intensive, repetitive and task-specific, to trigger neuroplasticity so the brain relearns tasks (speech, walking, reaching and grasping).
  • Assist-as-needed (AAN): copied from therapists' "active assist exercise". Help the patient complete the movement with the minimum assistance possible, allowing position and timing errors, because making errors drives motor learning while fixed guidance hampers it. Aim: minimise robot effort and maximise patient effort.
  • AAN vs impedance control: impedance control just makes the robot compliant so the patient can deviate from a fixed trajectory. AAN actively minimises support while still making sure the task gets done, giving spatial and temporal freedom.
  • AAN as optimisation (Emken 2005, gait swing phase): minimise J = ½(xi+1 − xd)² + (λR/2)Ri+1², where x is step height, R is peak robot force and λR weights how much robot force costs relative to error.
  • PASCAL robot: an end-effector robot with 3 active DoF for children with cerebral palsy, training reach and grasp toward on-screen targets. Its AAN path controller has four features:
    1. Virtual tunnel around the path, with a dead band of radius Rw. Outside it, a wall force (impedance control) pushes the arm back towards the nearest point on the path.
    2. Minimum and maximum speed walls (minimum-jerk profiles). They push a patient who is too slow and limit one who is too fast (e.g. falling under gravity).
    3. Direction-dependent supportive flux: fflux = kflux·tflux along the path tangent, with the gain set by the therapist for mildly affected patients. The total force is the flux plus the tunnel force.
    4. Gain scheduling: if the target isn't reached, wait 2 s after the minimum-speed wall arrives, then increase the stiffness and shrink Rw until the hand reaches the target or the maximum stiffness is hit.
  • Evaluation: soft tunnel walls give large deviations, while stiffer walls and a smaller Rw keep trajectories close to the path.
  • Why active assist works: effort provokes plasticity; stretching prevents soft-tissue stiffening and spasticity; novel somatosensory input helps induce plasticity; and the patient learns the movement pattern.
  • Downside: slacking. Humans let the robot do the work, which hinders learning.
  • Challenge-based alternatives: error augmentation (amplifying curvature errors with force fields made chronic stroke patients move straighter afterwards; Abdollahi 2014); motor exploration force fields; matching training to skill level and task; user-intention control (e.g. an EMG-controlled soft glove).

Go-to-market challenges (3 marks): pick any three and explain each in a line

  • Regulatory approval: FDA 510(k)/De Novo/PMA, CE mark under the MDR, a quality management system (ISO 13485), risk management (ISO 14971). Slow and expensive.
  • Clinical evidence: trials (often RCTs) to prove safety, efficacy and outcomes; ethics approval; recruiting patients.
  • Reimbursement: getting insurers or health systems to pay (billing codes such as remote patient monitoring); without it clinicians won't adopt the device.
  • Funding: long development timelines, investment rounds before any revenue (the "valley of death").
  • IP and freedom to operate: patents (e.g. PCT filings), protecting the invention while avoiding infringement (this was Kent Lee's area).
  • Manufacturing and scale-up: design for manufacture, supply chain, quality at volume, cost.
  • Clinical adoption and workflow: clinicians must trust the device and it must fit existing workflow ("engineers need clinicians"); training, sales channels and distribution partners.
  • User acceptance: comfort and usability (over 35% of orthoses are abandoned), compliance, and data privacy.

2025

Q1. Three requirements for a device that physically interacts with a human (3)

Any three, each with a short reason: inherent safety (low voltage, fail-safes, E-stop, limited forces); compliance rather than stiffness, so loads on the body are safe; backdrivability, so the user can move it; light weight, ergonomics and comfort; ease of donning and doffing; aesthetics; kinematic compatibility with human joints (avoiding misalignment).

Q2. SEA vs VSA (2)

An SEA places a spring of fixed stiffness in series between the motor and the load. It gives compliance, shock tolerance and force sensing, but its natural frequency can't be changed. A VSA has an elastic element whose stiffness can be adjusted (antagonistic, structural, mechanical or equilibrium-controlled), so compliance and resonance can be adapted to the task.

Q3. Two bio-inspired actuators and applications (4)

A pneumatic artificial muscle: a bladder in a braided mesh that contracts under pressure, light and compliant, used in agonist–antagonist pairs. Application: the Shadow dexterous hand, or a pneumatic-muscle gait-rehabilitation exoskeleton. Shape memory alloy wire (NiTi): contracts through a martensite→austenite phase change when heated, with a high force-to-weight ratio and biocompatibility. Application: an SMA-actuated prosthetic hand or wrist exoskeleton. Also acceptable: an IPMC (low-voltage bending) micropump or cell manipulator; a dielectric elastomer actuator or sensor.

Q4. Variable damping actuator and a real application (4)

An MR-fluid damper: iron micro-particles in oil, whose viscosity rises when a magnetic field from an electromagnet is applied, so damping is electrically controllable in milliseconds. Application: an MR prosthetic knee (Herr and Wilkenfeld; Össur Rheo Knee). An angle sensor and strain gauges detect the gait phase; a state machine sets a different damping in each of 5 states (high in stance for stability, low in pre-swing, adapted to walking speed in swing), and it adapts to the user after about 20 steps. The result is a natural, stable, energy-efficient gait with low power use. Other examples: a seat-suspension damper, or adjustable exercise resistance in a rehabilitation device.

Q5. Why traditional motion control isn't suitable (2)

A high-gain position controller is stiff: it tracks its own trajectory and rejects external forces. When a person moves differently or pushes back, it fights them, producing large, unsafe interaction forces and discomfort. It also assumes a known, predictable environment, while biomechatronic devices contact soft and hard objects and people whose behaviour changes. Force or interaction (impedance/admittance) needs to be controlled instead.

Q6. Why model and compensate system dynamics (3)

The model-based part of a partitioned controller (f = m·f′ + bẋ + kx) cancels the plant's inertia, friction or damping and stiffness, so the system behaves like a unit mass. The servo gains can then be tuned independently of the plant (e.g. for critical damping) and track accurately with lower gains. Gravity compensation removes the weight of the device and limb, so the patient only feels the intended assistance. That gives better transparency, safer and more compliant interaction, and less feedback effort.

Q7. Two AAN features for patients relearning to walk (4)

(1) A virtual tunnel / dead band around the desired foot path: no force while the foot is within Rw of the path, and an impedance-controlled wall force pushing it back when it strays. This allows spatial freedom and errors while guaranteeing a safe step. (2) Minimum and maximum speed walls (minimum-jerk timing): help only if the patient falls behind, and resist if they go too fast. (3) Supportive flux along the path, with a gain the therapist sets. (4) Gain scheduling: raise the stiffness only if the target isn't reached after a delay. Or optimisation-based AAN (Emken): minimise J = ½(step-height error)² + (λ/2)(robot force)², so the robot force decays as the patient improves. All of these maximise patient effort, which drives neuroplasticity, and counter slacking.

2024

Q2. Why variable stiffness/damping is desirable (2)

It lets the device adapt its interaction to the task and the user: compliant for safety, comfort and shock absorption, stiff for precision and support. It can store and release energy and tune resonance for efficiency (as muscles do in gait), and damping can stabilise a low-stiffness system, shorten settling time or add exercise resistance.

Q3. Example of a VSA and implementation (4)

An antagonistic VSA: two motors, each driving a non-linear (quadratic) spring on either side of a joint, like the biceps and triceps. Moving both motors the same way changes the joint position; co-contracting (pre-tensioning both) raises the joint stiffness. Implementation: an ankle prosthesis or knee exoskeleton, stiff in stance for support and compliant in swing or on impact, storing energy at heel strike and releasing it at push-off, with stiffness set by the controller from the gait phase. Alternatives: a mechanically controlled VS-Joint (cam and rollers changing the spring preload) in a safe collaborative arm, or structure-controlled stiffness by rotating a leaf spring.

Q4. Three bio-inspired actuators and applications (6)

Pneumatic muscle → Shadow Hand or gait exoskeleton. SMA → prosthetic hand or wrist exoskeleton. EAP (IPMC) → micropump, cell micromanipulator; dielectric elastomer → soft actuator or stretch sensor. Soft fluidic actuator → 3-D-printed prosthetic finger or soft rehabilitation glove. Give one line on how each works and one on why it suits the application.

Q5. Two interaction controller architectures (2)

Impedance control (motion in, force out: stable with stiff environments, less accurate in free space) and admittance control (force in, motion out: accurate in free space, can be unstable against stiff environments). Hybrid force/position control is another acceptable answer.

Q6. How AAN delivers health outcomes (5)

Rehabilitation must be intensive, repetitive and task-specific, with active participation to trigger neuroplasticity. AAN gives the minimum help needed to complete the movement, so the patient does as much as possible. It allows spatial and timing errors, which drive motor learning (while fixed guidance hampers it), and it counters slacking. It's implemented with a virtual tunnel with a dead band, speed walls, supportive flux and gain scheduling (PASCAL), or as an optimisation trading off error against robot force (Emken). As the patient improves, assistance fades, so the outcome tracks recovery.

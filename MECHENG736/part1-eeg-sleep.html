L1

Sleep, the PSG and the EEG

high MCQ weight

Measuring sleep

  • Polysomnogram (PSG) = EEG (brain) + EOG (eye movements) + EMG (muscle tone). Clinicians also record respiration (nasal airflow) and oxygen saturation, because sleep disorders often involve disrupted breathing.
  • Sleep definition: "a reversible state of perceptual disengagement from, and unresponsiveness to, the environment."
  • Sleep = REM + non-REM. The NREM stages can only be determined with EEG.
  • Behavioural signs: recumbence, quiescence, eye closure. EMG shows muscle tone loss is nearly complete in REM.
  • Physiological signs: autonomic changes (heart and respiration rate, blood pressure, body temperature).

Where EEG comes from

  • Generated by pyramidal neurons in cortical layers 3 and 5, similarly oriented, near the scalp.
  • It's the summed postsynaptic potentials (EPSPs/IPSPs) of thousands of cells, not action potentials (those are too short to record).
  • Each scalp electrode picks up at least about 6 cm² of synchronous cortex.
  • The summed activity behaves as a dipole parallel to the pyramidal cells.
  • Rhythmic EEG reflects cortex ↔ thalamus loops at rest. When the cortex is given a task it desynchronises, giving lower-amplitude, faster rhythms. Example: the posterior dominant rhythm (alpha, about 8.5–12 Hz) disappears when the eyes open.

History (dates are MCQ bait)

YearWhoWhat

The night's structure

  • Sleep is entered through NREM.
  • NREM–REM cycle ≈ 90–110 min, so about 4–6 cycles a night. Answer "5" if asked.
  • Slow-wave sleep dominates early in the night; REM dominates late.
  • Wake < 5%. Stage 1 2–5%, Stage 2 45–55%, Stage 3 3–8%, Stage 4 10–15%, REM 20–25% in 4–6 episodes. NREM is therefore about 75–80%.
  • Arousal threshold (how strong a stimulus it takes to wake you) rises from stage 1 to stage 4 and is variable in REM.

Animals, cycles, theories

  • All mammals and birds studied have REM + NREM, except cetaceans (dolphins, whales) and fur seals, which show unihemispheric NREM sleep.
  • All animals studied have a circadian activity cycle, even cockroaches.
  • Ultradian = period shorter than a day but longer than an hour (the 90-min sleep cycle). Circadian = about 24 h, persists without light cues (the sleep episode). So sleep episodes are circadian and sleep cycles are ultradian.
  • Sleep deprivation kills rats; fragmented sleep hurts human performance. Why we sleep is unknown.
  • Restorative theories, e.g. the memory consolidation hypothesis, are tested with sleep-deprivation studies. Adaptive theories, e.g. the protection hypothesis (keeps diurnal animals away from night-time predators and injury), are hard or impossible to test.
Traps: EEG reflects postsynaptic potentials, not action potentials. REM ≈ 20–25% (not 40%, not 12–14%). Stage 2 is the largest stage. Slow-wave sleep is early in the night, not late.

Staging rules in one list

  • Eyes closed before sleep: alpha.
  • N1: alpha replaced by LAMF. This is the accepted marker of sleep onset. Slow eye movements also appear.
  • N2: spindles and/or K-complexes.
  • N3 (stages 3+4, "slow-wave sleep"): high-voltage 0.5–2 Hz slow waves, 20–50% of the epoch = stage 3, > 50% = stage 4.
  • REM: LAMF EEG + low EMG + rapid eye movements. Fast, desynchronised EEG. Shorthand: "an active brain in a paralysed body". NREM is "a relatively inactive brain in a movable body".

Limitations of staging

  • Sleep onset isn't a single event, and no measurement is clear-cut every time. The consensus is that the EEG change to stage 1 marks onset. EEG is scored in 30-s epochs, which limits precision.
  • Inter-rater agreement (Cohen's kappa): 0.21–0.4 fair, 0.41–0.6 moderate, 0.61–0.8 substantial, 0.81–0.99 almost perfect. Human scorers reach "substantial". Agreement is highest for wake, REM and N2, then stages 3/4, and lowest for stage 1.
  • Thalamus: relays all sensory input except smell, plus motor signals, to the cortex.

In-lecture MCQs and their answers

  • Correct statement about young adults: NREM ≈ 80% of total sleep (not a 60-min cycle, not 9–10 REM episodes, and SWS is not in the last third).
  • First hour of sleep: mostly stages 3 and 4.
  • Bed 11 pm–7 am, most likely in stage 4 at: midnight.
  • Sensory processing during sleep: continues, but at a lower level than when awake.
  • Arousal threshold is highest in stage 4.
Traps: Spindles and K-complexes are N2 only, not REM. "Spindles" is a waveform, not a stage. Slow waves come from the cortex without the thalamus; spindles come from the thalamus. At sleep onset the EEG goes from alpha to LAMF, not the other way round.
L3

Sleep disorders and sleep apnoea

high MCQ weight

What changes sleep architecture

  • Age is the strongest and most consistent factor. Stages 3/4 peak in young children and fall markedly with age. Arousals increase with age. REM % holds up well into healthy old age.
  • Recovery sleep after sleep loss favours stages 3 and 4.
  • Circadian rhythm, temperature, drugs and sleep disorders also matter.

The four disorders

DisorderWhat it isPrevalence
NarcolepsyExtreme tendency to fall asleep in quiet or monotonous settings. Easily woken and immediately alert. Often with cataplexy and hypnagogic hallucinations.1 in 2,000 (0.05%)
Restless legs / periodic leg movementUnease, itching, twitching in the calves in bed at night; only relieved by moving the legs≈ 8%

Increasing prevalence: narcolepsy < RLS < OSA < insomnia. About 1 in 5 people has a chronic sleep disorder. Epidemiology is the study of incidence, prevalence and control of disease.

Apnoea

  • OSA: effort to breathe is frustrated by obstruction. Comes with loud snoring and daytime sleepiness (hypersomnia).
  • CSA (central): no effort to breathe, no obstruction, no loud snoring. Caused by cardiac or neurological disorders, usually in elderly people.
  • AHI = average number of apnoea + hypopnea events (each > 10 s) per hour. < 5 normal · 5–15 mild · 15–30 moderate · > 30 severe.
  • What OSA looks like on a PSG: no airflow; thorax and abdomen moving out of phase (paradoxical breathing); oxygen desaturation; progressive tachycardia on ECG; an EEG arousal ends the event, then breathing and saturation recover.
  • Prevalence rises with age; by about 50, women catch up with men. Higher in minority populations. There is a genetic component (inherited airway anatomy).
  • Risk factors: excess weight, older age, narrow airway, hypertension, chronic nasal congestion, smoking, diabetes, male sex, family history, asthma. Not dehydration.
  • Symptoms: excessive sleepiness (most common; 15–50% of screened cases), low energy, waking up gasping or choking, snoring (sensitive but not specific), nocturia (> 2 times a night), morning headache, nocturnal reflux, witnessed apnoeas. Not the hypnic jerk.
  • Treatment: behavioural (no alcohol, exercise, weight loss, avoid sleeping on your back); devices (CPAP, which keeps the airway open, and oral mandibular repositioning devices); surgery in severe cases when CPAP fails (e.g. soft-palate resection). CPAP is not the only treatment. Successful CPAP can cause SWS or REM rebound.
  • Wearables for OSA screening: bracelet (PPG: HR, SpO₂, respiration), chest band (ECG), adhesive patch (respiration, accelerometer), ring, headset (EEG), EMG.
Traps: AHI 25 is moderate, not severe. AHI < 5 is not mild OSA. CSA has no airway obstruction, so soft-palate anatomy is an OSA risk, not a CSA risk. Males are at greater OSA risk. SWS goes down with age.
L4

HCI/BCI, pattern recognition and Fisher LDA

short-answer core
  • HCI loop: input → signal processing → decision / control logic → output → feedback. Example: an OSA wristband goes PPG → artefact removal + feature extraction → classification → record OSA events → OSA report.
  • BCI = a direct communication pathway between the brain and an external device.
  • Pattern recognition pipeline: input → sense → segment → extract features → classify → post-process → decision. That's 5 stages.
  • Classification is a statistical process that sorts observations into classes. You can use it for inference ("are A and B reliably different?") or prediction/decoding (label unseen data). It's statistical because features vary within a class, so accuracy is rarely 100%.
  • Feature extraction is domain-dependent and needs specialist knowledge. Good features are similar within a class, different between classes, and invariant to irrelevant changes. Classifiers are usually domain-independent.
  • A feature vector lives in an n-D feature space (also called vector or embedding space). Linearly separable means a hyperplane separates the classes perfectly. Data can be separable but not linearly separable, in which case you need a non-linear boundary.
  • Linear boundary in 2-D: a·x₁ + b·x₂ + c = 0. Quadratic: f + a x₁ + b x₂ + c x₁² + d x₁x₂ + e x₂² = 0.
  • Linear classifiers: LDA, logistic regression, linear SVM. Non-linear: kernel SVM, decision trees and random forests, neural networks (MLP, CNN, Transformer).
  • The design process (collect data → features → model → train → evaluate) is heuristic, meaning trial and error, and you loop back.

Fisher's LDA, step by step

  1. Project the data onto a direction w. Choose w to maximise the separation of the projected means and minimise the within-class scatter: 
    J(w) = (m₂ − m₁)² / (s₁² + s₂²)          m, s = projected mean and scatter
  2. The solution: 
    μᵢ = (1/nᵢ) Σ xᵢ                       class mean
Sᵢ = Σ (x − μᵢ)(x − μᵢ)ᵀ                 class scatter matrix (2×2)
S  = S₁ + S₂                             pooled within-class scatter
w  = S⁻¹ (μ₂ − μ₁)                       points roughly from μ₁ to μ₂
  3. Threshold: if the projected classes are normal with equal scatter (and equal priors), the error-minimising boundary is perpendicular to w and passes through the midpoint of the means: 
    wᵀx + b = 0      with   b = −wᵀ(μ₁ + μ₂)/2
wᵀx + b > 0  → class 2        wᵀx + b < 0  → class 1
Shortcut: if both classes have identity (or equal spherical) covariance, then S⁻¹ ∝ I, so w ∝ μ₂ − μ₁ and the boundary is the perpendicular bisector of the line joining the two means. That's what the 2025 question tested.
Trap: Fisher LDA uses every point, through the means and scatter. Removing a single observation does change the fit, unlike an SVM, where only the support vectors matter.
L5

Prediction and FFT band-power features

medium
  • Prediction/decoding: record a new 5-s epoch, compute its features, place it in the feature space, and check which side of the boundary it falls on, using the sign of wᵀx + b.
  • Band-power features via FFT (course convention): 
    X[k] = (1/N) Σ x[n] e^(−j2πkn/N)           # np.fft.fft(x)/N
Δf   = fs / N                                 # frequency resolution
P[k] = |X[k]|²; one-sided: keep 0…N/2, double every bin except DC
P_band = Σ P[k] for f_k in [f_lo, f_hi]
  • For a sine of amplitude A: the two-sided |X| at ±f is A/2, the one-sided amplitude is A, and the one-sided power is A²/2.
  • Bands: theta 4–8 Hz, alpha 8–12/13 Hz, beta 12/13–30 Hz. Eyes closed means more alpha power; beta and theta barely change.
  • sklearn: LinearDiscriminantAnalysis().fit(X, y) then .predict(X_new). MATLAB: classify(sample, training, group, type) with type 'linear' (pooled full covariance), 'diaglinear' (pooled diagonal covariance, i.e. features assumed independent) or 'quadratic'.
  • Vibe coding, agents and harnesses are non-examinable.
L6

Overfitting, hold-out and cross-validation

medium
  • Overfitting: a model becomes needlessly complex and "fits the noise", so it predicts new data worse than a simpler model. Lecture example: data generated by y = 2x + noise. Model A (2 parameters, linear) vs Model B (9 parameters, 8th-order polynomial). Model B fits the training points better but predicts badly.
  • LOOCV results: Model A mean predictive SSE 39.5 (SD 45.5) vs Model B 891.3 (SD 3236.8). Model A is the better model.
  • Ideally you'd test on freshly generated data, but in practice you don't have it. So you keep a hold-out sample that isn't used for training. One hold-out split can be biased by which points land in it.
  • k-fold CV: 
    partition data into k groups of equal size
loop k times
  hold out a group not previously held out
  fit / train on the remaining ("held-in") groups
  score the prediction of the held-out group
end
evaluate = average of the k scores
  • k can be anything from 2 to N. k = N is leave-one-out (LOOCV).
  • Stratified random sampling: each split takes roughly equal proportions from each class, which reduces bias.
  • Use a single hold-out when the dataset is huge and refitting is too expensive (e.g. training an LLM). EEG datasets are small, so k-fold is preferred.
  • Repeated k-fold: run k-fold n times with different random splits for a more robust estimate.
  • CV can be done at sample, subject (leave-one-subject-out) or dataset level. Subject- or dataset-level CV tests generalisability across people or datasets.
L7

Comparing classifiers, chance level and class imbalance

new this year

Is A really better than B?

  • McNemar's test (single hold-out, or LOOCV, since each fold tests one sample and so gives paired predictions). Build a 2×2 table of A correct/wrong × B correct/wrong. Only the discordant cells matter: n₀₁ (A right, B wrong) and n₁₀ (A wrong, B right). 
    χ² = (|n₀₁ − n₁₀| − 1)² / (n₀₁ + n₁₀)       ~ χ² with 1 dof under H₀
reject H₀ at α = 0.05 if χ² > 3.841
  • Paired t-test on k-fold differences: t = mean(d) / (std(d)/√k). It's flawed because the fold training sets overlap, so the differences are not independent. The variance is underestimated, t is inflated, and you get too many false positives (rejecting a true H₀).
  • Corrected repeated k-fold CV test (Nadeau & Bengio 2003). Recommended by Bouckaert & Frank (2004) as the most replicable. 
    t_corr = mean(d_ij) / √[ (1/(n·k) + n_test/n_train) · s² ]
  • Null hypothesis: no difference, so results are down to chance. p-value: the probability of getting results at least as extreme by chance alone.

Is it better than chance?

  • Accuracy above theoretical chance (e.g. 55% vs 50%) is not the same as being significantly above chance.
  • Permutation test: cross-validate on the true labels to get a₀. Then B times: shuffle the labels, cross-validate again, record a*. p = (#{a* ≥ a₀} + 1)/(B + 1). Use B = 1000 if you can and at least 100.
  • With imbalanced classes (90/10 medical screening, 98/2 fraud), a dummy classifier that always picks the majority class scores 80–95% and is useless.

Metrics

Predicted 0Predicted 1
True 0 (negative)TNFP
True 1 (positive)FNTP
  • Accuracy = (TP+TN)/total. Use it for balanced classes with equal error costs.
  • Sensitivity / recall / TPR = TP/(TP+FN). Use it when the positive class is rare but important (disease screening).
  • Specificity = TN/(TN+FP). The slides pair it with a rare negative class (their example is fraud detection).
  • ROC: y = TPR, x = FPR = 1 − specificity = FP/(FP+TN). Sweep each unique score as a threshold (predict 1 if score ≥ t). AUC 0.5 = random, 1.0 = perfect, whatever the class proportions, so use it for imbalanced data.

G · Short-answer patterns from 2023

Context: two LDA models on alpha vs beta power. Sample 1 is elongated along a diagonal (alpha and beta correlated). Model 1's boundary is nearly horizontal; model 2's is tilted.

Model answers
  • Linearly separable? Only if a straight line gets every point right. In that plot a few open and filled points overlap around (1, 2), so the samples are close to, but not perfectly, linearly separable. State your criterion explicitly.
  • Remove one observation, does the fit change? Yes, slightly. Fisher LDA is computed from every point's contribution to the class means and scatter matrices, so removing any point moves μ and S, and therefore w and the boundary.
  • Covariance assumptions: Model 1 assumed a diagonal covariance (alpha and beta independent, i.e. 'diaglinear'), giving a near-horizontal boundary. Model 2 assumed a full pooled covariance (alpha and beta correlated, 'linear'), so its boundary follows the diagonal elongation of sample 1.
  • CV pseudocode: split the data into k stratified folds. For each fold, train model 1 and model 2 on the other k−1 folds, test both on the held-out fold, and record accuracy. Average the k accuracies for each model. Use a corrected repeated k-fold t-test (or McNemar with LOOCV) to decide whether the difference is significant.
  • Which generalises better? Model 2. The data clearly show correlated features, so the full-covariance assumption matches how the data were generated, and its boundary should misclassify fewer new points.
